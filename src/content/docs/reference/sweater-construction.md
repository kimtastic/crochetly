Sweater construction determines how the pieces fit together, how the garment drapes, and how easy the pattern is to write and follow. Each method has trade-offs between fit, complexity, and design possibilities.

## Raglan sweater

Raglan sleeves are seamed diagonally from the underarm to the neckline. The shoulder seam extends into the body in a diagonal line. Raglans are most often worked top-down in the round.

**Fit:** Comfortable and accommodating. The diagonal seam creates a softer shoulder line. Fits a wide range of body types without precise shoulder shaping.

**Writing a raglan pattern:** The raglan increase rate determines yoke depth and final stitch count. Plan the increase schedule so the yoke reaches the desired depth and the sleeve and body counts match the target measurements at the same time.

- Increases are worked at four raglan seam lines, two per sleeve (front and back).
- Each increase round adds 8 stitches (or 4 for cardigans worked flat).
- Increase every round or every other round depending on the desired yoke depth and row gauge.
- The raglan seam can be a single stitch, a cable, or a gusset.

**Best for:** Pullovers and cardigans for all body types. Raglans are beginner-friendly and forgiving.

## Circular yoke sweater

The yoke is worked in one piece in the round, with increases distributed evenly around the yoke rather than at seam lines. The result is a seamless garment with a round yoke decorated with stitch patterns or stranded colorwork.

**Fit:** The yoke width determines the neckline and shoulder fit. The increase distribution affects how the pattern sits on the shoulders. Circular yokes are wider across the shoulders than raglans for the same chest measurement.

**Writing a circular yoke pattern:** Increase round placement is critical. Evenly spaced increases maintain the stitch pattern and prevent puckering. The yoke chart must align with the increase rounds.

- Increases are often worked in a single round (sometimes 2-3 increase rounds total) rather than gradually.
- Stitch count per increase round equals the number of stitches at that point divided by the increase interval.
- The chart repeats must work with the stitch count at each stage.
- Short rows at the back neck improve fit by lowering the front relative to the back.

**Best for:** Colorwork patterns, fair isle sweaters, and designs where a seamless look matters. The circular yoke is a classic Nordic construction method.

## Drop shoulder sweater

The body and sleeves are straight rectangles seamed together at the shoulder. The sleeve cap is minimal or nonexistent; the sleeve width matches the armhole opening.

**Fit:** Relaxed and boxy. The straight shoulder line can pull across the upper back if the chest is large relative to the shoulders. Drop shoulders work best for casual, oversized fits.

**Writing a drop shoulder pattern:** The simplest construction to grade. Body and sleeve widths are straight measurements. The armhole depth is simply the sleeve width.

- No armhole or sleeve cap shaping required.
- The shoulder seam sits on top of the arm, not at the shoulder bone.
- Seamed or seamless: can be worked flat in pieces or in the round with steeked armholes.
- Sleeve ease is minimal; the sleeve width matches the armhole opening.

**Best for:** Bulky or chunky sweaters, casual designs, and patterns where ease of construction matters more than tailored fit.

## Set-in sleeve sweater

The sleeve cap is curved to fit into a shaped armhole. This is the same construction used in commercial sewing.

**Fit:** The most tailored and fitted option. The curved sleeve cap follows the natural shoulder line. Set-in sleeves work well for fitted garments and dressier designs.

**Writing a set-in sleeve pattern:** Requires the most shaping of any construction. The armhole depth, armhole curve, and sleeve cap height must match exactly.

- Armhole depth is usually 6-9 in (15-23 cm) depending on size and fit.
- Sleeve cap height is typically 35-50% of the armhole depth.
- Bind-off and decrease rates at the armhole and sleeve cap must align.
- Often worked flat in pieces and seamed.

**Best for:** Fitted garments, dress sweaters, designs with tailored shaping, and patterns where the sleeve is a different stitch pattern from the body.

## Contiguous sweater

The sleeve and body are worked in one piece with increases at the shoulder points, similar to raglan but without visible seam lines. The shoulder is shaped with short rows.

**Fit:** Similar to raglan but with a smoother shoulder line. The contiguous method creates a gentle slope from the neck to the shoulder.

**Writing a contiguous pattern:** The increase pattern is more complex than raglan because increases happen at the shoulder points rather than along seam lines.

- Short rows shape the shoulder slope before the sleeve and body are separated.
- Fewer total increase points than raglan.
- Requires careful stitch count tracking.

**Best for:** Seamless garments where a clean, modern look is desired and the designer wants an alternative to raglan or circular yoke.

## How do I choose a construction method?

| Fit precision | Moderate | Moderate | Relaxed | High | Moderate |
| Beginner friendly | Yes | Moderate | Yes | No | No |
| Colorwork friendly | Limited | Yes | Moderate | Moderate | Limited |
| Seamless | Yes | Yes | Optional | No | Yes |
| Grading difficulty | Moderate | Moderate | Easy | Hard | Hard |
| Best stitch pattern | Simple | Colorwork | Any | Any | Simple |

## What general tips apply to sweater construction?

Sweater fit depends on more than chest circumference. Shoulder width, armhole depth, and sleeve cap height all affect how the garment drapes. Swatch and block before calculating final dimensions.

- **Ease varies by construction:** Raglan and drop shoulder sweaters need less ease than set-in sleeve sweaters for the same fit feel.
- **Row gauge matters for yokes:** A half-stitch difference in row gauge can shift the yoke depth by an inch or more.
- **Shoulder width:** If the shoulder width is narrower than the wearers shoulder bone, the sleeve will pull at the armhole. Measure cross back width carefully.
- **Sleeve cap ease:** For set-in sleeves, the sleeve cap circumference should be 10-20% larger than the armhole circumference to create a smooth cap.
- **Short rows improve fit:** Back neck short rows raise the back neckline relative to the front. This prevents the front from riding up and is worth the extra complexity.
