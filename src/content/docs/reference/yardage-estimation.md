---
title: Yardage estimation for patterns
description: How to estimate yarn yardage per size when writing knitting and crochet patterns, including methods based on stitch count, swatch weight, and project type, with tips for accuracy
---

Yardage estimates let you tell makers how much yarn to buy for each size. Accurate estimates reduce waste and prevent running short. This guide covers several methods and how to present yardage in your pattern.

## Why does yardage matter in patterns?

Makers rely on yardage to buy yarn before starting. An underestimate means running out mid-project. An overestimate means extra cost and leftovers.

For patterns with multiple sizes, yardage changes with each size. The difference between the smallest and largest size can be 40% or more of the total yardage.

## How do I estimate yardage for a pattern?

There are three reliable methods. Each works best for different situations.

### Method 1: Knit or crochet a sample and weigh it

This is the most accurate method. Knit or crochet a swatch of the main stitch pattern, measure its dimensions, and weigh it.

1. Knit or crochet a swatch at least 6 x 6 in (15 x 15 cm).
2. Block and dry the swatch.
3. Measure the exact dimensions.
4. Weigh the swatch in grams.
5. Calculate grams per square inch (or per 10 x 10 cm).
6. Multiply by the total fabric area for each size.

**Example:** A 6 x 6 in swatch weighs 12 g. That is 0.33 g per square inch. A size M sweater with 1200 square inches of fabric needs about 400 g of yarn.

This method works for any stitch pattern and yarn combination. It accounts for the real fabric density after blocking.

### Method 2: Calculate from stitch count

If you know the yarn weight per stitch from a published source or previous samples, you can estimate yardage from total stitch count.

1. Calculate total stitches for each size: stitches per row times number of rows (for flat pieces) or total rounds times stitches per round (for in-the-round pieces).
2. Add a percentage for bind-offs, seams, and edges (typically 3-5%).
3. Divide by the stitches per yard from your gauge swatch.

| Yarn weight | Approximate stitches per yard (stockinette) | Approximate stitches per meter (stockinette) |
|---|---|---|
| Fingering / Sock | 40-50 sts per yd | 44-55 sts per m |
| Sport / DK | 28-35 sts per yd | 31-38 sts per m |
| Worsted / Aran | 18-25 sts per yd | 20-27 sts per m |
| Bulky / Chunky | 12-16 sts per yd | 13-17 sts per m |

These numbers change with stitch pattern. Cables use more yarn per stitch. Lace uses less.

### Method 3: Use an existing similar pattern as reference

If you have written a similar pattern in the same yarn weight and size range, use its yardage as a starting point and adjust for differences.

- Add 10-15% for longer sleeves or body.
- Subtract 10-15% for cropped lengths.
- Add 20-30% for textured stitch patterns (cables, bobbles, slip stitch).
- Subtract 10-20% for openwork or lace.

This is the least accurate method but works well for experienced designers who have a library of past projects.

## How do I present yardage per size in a pattern?

The clearest format is a table showing yardage (or skein count) for each size.

```plaintext
| Size | Yardage (yds) | Yardage (m) | Skeins |
|------|---------------|-------------|--------|
| XS   | 700           | 640         | 4      |
| S    | 775           | 709         | 4      |
| M    | 825           | 754         | 5      |
| L    | 875           | 800         | 5      |
| XL   | 925           | 846         | 6      |
| 2XL  | 1000          | 914         | 6      |
| 3XL  | 1050          | 960         | 7      |
| 4XL  | 1100          | 1006        | 7      |
| 5XL  | 1200          | 1097        | 8      |
```

Round yardage to the nearest 25 yards (or 25 meters) for readability. Include both yards and meters if your pattern is sold internationally.

## What factors affect yardage?

Several factors shift yardage beyond the base stitch count.

**Stitch pattern:** Textured stitches use more yarn. A sweater in moss stitch may need 15-20% more yarn than the same sweater in stockinette. Lace and openwork use less.

**Negative ease:** Garments with negative ease (socks, fitted sweaters) use less yarn because the fabric is stretched in wear. Base your yardage on the relaxed, not stretched, fabric dimensions.

**Modifications:** Longer hems, sleeves, or added details (hoods, pockets, collars) increase yardage. Note these as add-ons in the pattern so makers can plan.

**Yarn substitution:** Different brands of the same weight can have different yardage per gram. Always state yardage (length) not just skein count.

## How do I estimate yardage for colorwork?

Stranded colorwork uses more yarn than single-color knitting because of the floats on the back.

- Add 10-15% for simple two-color patterns with short floats.
- Add 15-25% for complex patterns with long floats.
- Work a colorwork swatch and weigh it rather than estimating from stitch count alone.
- Estimate each color separately if the pattern has large areas of a single color.

For crochet colorwork, the float penalty is smaller because the stitch structure is thicker. Add 5-10% for most stranded or intarsia crochet patterns.

## What if I cannot swatch every size?

If you only knit or crochet one sample, estimate yardage for other sizes by scaling proportionally.

- Yardage scales roughly with total fabric surface area, not just linear dimensions.
- A sweater that is 10% wider and 10% longer has about 20% more fabric area and needs about 20% more yarn.
- Round up for sizes you are less confident about.

**Example:** Your sample size M uses 825 yards. Size S is 5% narrower and 5% shorter in the body and sleeves, so estimate about 10% less fabric area and 10% less yardage (about 740 yards).

This is an approximation. If possible, have test knitters or crocheters track their yardage for the sizes you did not sample.
