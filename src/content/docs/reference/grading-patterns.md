title: Grading patterns across sizes
description: How to plan stitch counts, increase schedules, and length adjustments when writing multi-size knitting and crochet patterns, with methods for consistent sizing across a size range and guidance for size-inclusive grading
---

Grading is the process of scaling a pattern from one size to a range of sizes. A well-graded pattern fits consistently across all sizes, with the same ease, the same proportions, and the same stitch pattern alignment.

## What is pattern grading?

Grading means calculating stitch counts, row counts, and shaping instructions for each size in your pattern. It is not just scaling up or down by a fixed percentage. Different parts of the body grow at different rates across sizes.

For example, the chest circumference increases more between sizes than the shoulder width or the armhole depth. A pattern that scales chest circumference proportionally but ignores armhole depth will fit poorly at the extremes of the size range.

## What does size-inclusive grading mean?

Size-inclusive grading means offering a full range of sizes where every size gets the same quality of fit, not just the same stitch counts scaled up. A size-inclusive pattern typically fits full busts from at least 30 in (76 cm) to 60 in (152 cm), graded at consistent intervals throughout the range.

**The minimum standard:** A pattern should include sizes that fit full busts from 30 to 60 in (76 to 152 cm). Two common size series that achieve this are:

- 28, 32, 36, 40, 44, 48, 52, 56, 60, 64, 68 in, or
- 30, 34, 38, 42, 46, 50, 54, 58, 62, 66 in

Both series use a consistent 4 in (10 cm) increment between sizes. The increment must be the same throughout the range. If the smallest sizes are 2 in (5 cm) apart and the largest are 4 in (10 cm) apart, makers do not have equal access to a good fit.

**Consistent fit matters.** A size-inclusive pattern does not just add larger stitch counts. Every size must reproduce the proportions, ease, and visual effect of the sample. A pattern that offers extended sizes without adjusting for changing body proportions is not truly size-inclusive. It creates garments that fit poorly at the extremes of the range.

**Size vs. shape inclusion.** Size inclusion focuses on offering a full range of sizes. Shape inclusion goes further by addressing different body proportions (bust/waist/hip ratios, height, arm shape). Most makers will need some adjustments for a truly bespoke fit, but size-inclusive patterns should give every maker a strong starting point.

### Key grading concerns for size-inclusive patterns

**Garment weight:** As garments get larger they get heavier. A larger sweater stretches more in the body under its own weight, and the sleeves pull laterally on the neckline. Designers should consider whether a weighted gauge is needed for hanging pieces, and whether neck depth or width should be adjusted to account for the pull.

**Old ratios do not work at scale:** Traditional rules like "upper arm circumference should be X% of full bust" or "drop-shoulder body is a rectangle" break down when the size range extends past the middle sizes. In the smallest sizes, the shoulder seam sits too high and restricts movement. In the largest sizes, the seam drops toward the elbow and creates excess fabric. Grading must be based on actual body measurements at each fit point, not on ratios derived from bust size.

A drop-shoulder garment, for example, should grade its cross-shoulder width from the body's cross-shoulder measurement, not from half the full bust. Designers must identify key fit points (cross-shoulder, upper arm, armhole depth, bust) and grade each one individually.

**Schematic first, shaping second:** The shaping instructions are always in service of the desired final measurements. Determine the stitch counts and row counts that make up the schematic first, then figure out the increase or decrease schedules to reach them. A common mistake is to pick a familiar increase pattern (like "increase 8 stitches every other round") without checking whether it produces the right measurements for every size.

## How do I plan stitch counts for multiple sizes?

Start with the stitch repeat of your stitch or colorwork pattern. All sizes should be compatible with the repeat count.

1. **Choose a stitch repeat unit:** Determine the repeat count for your stitch pattern (e.g., a 6-stitch cable repeat, a 12-stitch colorwork motif).
2. **Determine the base size:** Pick a middle size and calculate the stitch count that fits the finished measurement at your gauge.
3. **Adjust up and down:** Add or subtract multiples of the stitch repeat to reach the next size. Adjust within the repeat as needed using fractional repeats at edges or partial repeats at seams.
4. **Check the shaping:** Verify that the increase and decrease counts work with the stitch pattern. Avoid placing decreases in the middle of a cable or colorwork motif.

**Example:** A raglan sweater with a 12-stitch colorwork repeat. A medium size needs 240 stitches (20 repeats). The next size up needs 252 stitches (21 repeats). The smallest size needs 216 stitches (18 repeats).

If a size falls between repeat multiples, add partial repeats at the sides or adjust the ease slightly. A 2-stitch difference is often acceptable and can be handled at the seam or raglan line.

## How do I plan increase schedules?

Increases (or decreases) must produce the right stitch count at the right depth. In raglan and circular yoke patterns, the increase rate determines the yoke depth.

### For raglan sweaters

Raglan sweaters increase 8 stitches per increase round (4 seams x 2 increases per seam). The increase interval (every round, every other round, or a mix) determines the yoke depth.

- **Yoke depth target:** measure from cast-on to underarm at the front.
- **Rounds needed:** yoke depth (in inches) divided by row gauge (in inches per round).
- **Increases needed:** (target stitch count at yoke minus starting stitch count) divided by 8.
- **Increase interval:** rounds needed divided by increase repeats.

If the increase interval is a fraction (e.g., 3.4 rounds between increases), alternate between 3 and 4 rounds between increases for a smooth shape.

**Example:** A raglan needs 200 stitches total at the yoke from a starting 80 stitches. That is 120 increases, or 15 increase rounds (120 / 8). If the yoke needs to be 8 in deep at 5 rows per inch (40 rounds total), work an increase round every 2.67 rounds. Alternate every 2 rounds and every 3 rounds.

### For circular yoke sweaters

Circular yokes increase on specific chart rounds. The increase amount per round depends on the number of stitches at that point.

- **Increase round count:** typically 2-5 increase rounds total, depending on the design.
- **Stitches per increase:** choose a number that divides evenly into the current stitch count.
- **Spacing:** increases are spread evenly around the yoke. Place them between colorwork motifs or at natural break points in the chart.

**Example:** A yoke with 240 stitches needs to increase to 360 stitches. That is 120 increases. If you use 3 increase rounds, work 40 increases per round (every 6 stitches: k4, m1, repeat).

### For drop shoulder and set-in sleeve sweaters

These constructions have fewer or no yoke increases. The body and sleeves are calculated from circumference measurements directly.

- **Body stitches:** finished chest circumference (with ease) x gauge.
- **Sleeve stitches:** finished upper arm circumference (with ease) x gauge.
- **No intra-yoke increases** for drop shoulder. Set-in sleeves need armhole and sleeve cap decreases, which are calculated from armhole depth and sleeve cap height.

## How do I handle length adjustments across sizes?

Length does not scale proportionally with circumference. A child size needs proportionally more length relative to width than an adult size.

### Account for underarm depth

A common grading error is using a fixed underarm-to-hem or underarm-to-cuff length for all sizes. Underarm depth increases as body size increases. A size chart typically adds 0.25-0.5 in (0.5-1 cm) of underarm depth per size. If you add that underarm depth AND use the same body/sleeve length from the underarm down for every size, the total length gets longer with each size.

The correct approach: start with the desired total length for each size, then subtract the underarm depth to get the underarm-to-hem length. This means underarm-to-hem gets slightly shorter as sizes get larger, keeping the total length consistent.

**Example:** A size M with 8 in (20 cm) underarm depth and a 16 in (40 cm) total body length works 8 in (20 cm) from underarm to hem. A size 2XL with 10 in (25 cm) underarm depth should work 6 in (15 cm) from underarm to hem to maintain the same 16 in (40 cm) total body length.

The same principle applies to sleeves: underarm depth and sleeve cap height increase with size, so underarm-to-cuff length must decrease to keep the total sleeve length consistent.

### Body length increments

Add length in small, consistent increments. A standard expectation on a 4 in (10 cm) chest grade is 0.25 in (0.5 cm) of additional body length per size, to account for the curve of the body at larger circumferences.

- **Children:** add 1-2 in (2.5-5 cm) per size for body length. Children grow faster in length than in width.
- **Adults:** add 0-0.25 in (0-0.5 cm) per size. The goal is consistent total length, not increasing length.
- **Extra large sizes:** the same 0-0.25 in (0-0.5 cm) applies. Total body length should not increase meaningfully across the size range.

### Sleeve length

Sleeve length should also be consistent across sizes. If the upper arm has positive ease, the sleeve does not need additional length in larger sizes. If the sleeve fits closely, a small increase of 0.25 in (0.5 cm) per size may be needed to accommodate the curve of the arm.

- **Children:** add 1-2 in (2.5-5 cm) per size for sleeve length.
- **Adults:** add 0-0.25 in (0-0.5 cm) per size, or none if the sleeve has positive ease.

If a sleeve has shaping decreases, spread them evenly over the sleeve length. Increase the even rounds between decrease rounds for longer sizes.

## How do I manage stitch pattern alignment across sizes?

Stitch patterns that cross size boundaries need careful planning.

**Centered motifs:** Place a single motif at the center front or center back. Add partial repeats on each side that are consistent across sizes. The partial repeats grow or shrink between sizes, while the center motif stays aligned.

**Full repeats:** If the pattern repeats fully around the body, adjust stitch count by multiples of the repeat. If a size falls between repeats, use partial repeats at the sides or underarm where they are least visible.

**Raglan seam patterns:** If the raglan seam has a design element (cable, garter panel, lace), its width is fixed across sizes. The body and sleeve panels on either side grow or shrink.

**Colorwork motifs:** Each size must fit a whole number of motif repeats. If sizes diverge, adjust the stitch count with plain rounds or partial motifs at the underarm.

## What is the size-inclusive standard?

A size-inclusive adult garment should fit full busts from at least 30 in (76 cm) to 60 in (152 cm), graded at consistent intervals.

The standard 4 in (10 cm) chest increment applies across the entire size range. If a stitch repeat forces a deviation, the gap should be consistent across all sizes, not concentrated at one end of the range.

| Demographic | Increment between sizes | Size-inclusive range |
|---|---|---|
| Children (0-10) | 2 in (5 cm) chest | 20-32 in (51-81 cm) |
| Youth (10-16) | 2 in (5 cm) chest | 30-40 in (76-102 cm) |
| Adult | 4 in (10 cm) chest | 30-60+ in (76-152+ cm) |

For adults, a 4 in (10 cm) increment is the standard. Some designers use a 2 in (5 cm) increment, especially for fitted garments. If you use a 2 in increment, use it consistently throughout the range. Do not switch to 4 in increments for larger sizes.

A pattern that covers both child and adult sizes may need different increment rates for each range. Label clearly where the transition occurs and ensure the ease recommendation is appropriate for each demographic.

## How do I scale design features across sizes?

Design features (motifs, cables, colorwork patterns, pockets) create a visual identity. They need the same care in grading as the fit measurements.

### All-over stitch patterns

Treat all-over stitch patterns like printed fabric. A cable repeat, lace pattern, or colorwork motif stays the same size across all sizes. The number of repeats changes, not the motif itself. This is the simplest approach and works for most patterns.

### Focal motifs

A single large motif (an intarsia heart, a central cable panel, a lace medallion) should be sized relative to the garment, not fixed. A 3 in (7.5 cm) heart that looks balanced on a size S will be proportionally small on a size 5XL.

For a focal motif, create 2-4 size groupings and scale the motif by adjusting the stitch count or chart. Tie the motif width to a percentage of the front width rather than a fixed stitch count.

### Placement

Place motifs using body landmarks, not fixed stitch counts. As body size increases, more of the circumference falls at the sides rather than the front. Tie the placement of a front motif to the bust apex width so it stays visually centered on the front of the body regardless of size.

For raglan or yoke elements, the placement relative to the raglan seam or center of the yoke should remain consistent across sizes.

### Fixed elements

Elements like button bands, pocket openings, and collar widths often work best at a fixed size across all sizes. A button band that is 1.5 in (4 cm) wide works at every size. Increase the number of buttons or the spacing between them, not the width of the band.

## How do I grade on the correct measurements?

Choosing the right body measurement to drive each garment measurement is critical for consistent fit across sizes.

**Cross-shoulder width:** Base this on the body's cross-shoulder measurement, not on half the full bust. In drop-shoulder garments especially, using half the full bust creates a shoulder seam that sits too high on small sizes and too low on large sizes.

**Neck depth:** The visual distance from the neckline to the collarbone or shoulder should be the same across sizes. A crew neck or deeper neckline does not need to change depth between sizes. Only very high necklines (jewel, funnel) may need minor adjustments.

**Neck width:** Increased slightly in larger sizes to accommodate a wider neck base, but the change is small, typically no more than 3 in (7.5 cm) total across the full size range.

**Wrist circumference:** Increased slightly in larger sizes, but the change is small, typically no more than 3 in (7.5 cm) across the full size range. Too-tight wrists are a common fit complaint in larger sizes.

**Armhole depth:** Increases with each size. This is one of the most important measurements for fit. A pattern that uses the same armhole depth for all sizes will bind at the underarm in larger sizes.

## How do I ensure consistent quality across all sizes?

A size-inclusive pattern does not just include more sizes. It gives every size the same standard of fit and finish.

- **Test at the extremes.** Have at least one test knitter or crocheter at the smallest size and one at the largest size. Mid-size testers do not reveal fit problems at the edges of the range.
- **Check the schematic for every size.** Do not assume that proportions that work at the sample size carry over. Review the schematic at each size independently.
- **Verify design feature proportions.** A motif that looks balanced at the sample size should look equally balanced at every size, not disproportionately large or small.
- **Write separate instructions when needed.** If the increase schedule, stitch count, or shaping differs meaningfully between sizes, write separate lines or sub-sections rather than relying on parenthetical notation alone. Clear instructions prevent errors.
- **Provide sizing guidance.** Help makers choose a size by explaining the intended ease, which measurements drive the fit, and when they may want to size up or down. See the [sizing style guide](/style guide/sizing/) for more.

## How do I present graded instructions in a pattern?

Use parentheses to separate sizes. List stitch counts and shaping instructions in size order.

```plaintext
CO 80 (88, 96, 104, 112) sts.
Increase rnd: K4 (5, 6, 5, 6), m1, *K8 (9, 10, 9, 10), m1; repeat from * to last 4 (5, 6, 5, 6) sts, k4 (5, 6, 5, 6). [40 (44, 48, 52, 56) sts increased]
```

Group similar sizes if the instructions diverge. Label clearly which instructions apply to which size.

```plaintext
Sizes: 0-6 mo (6-12 mo, 1-2 yr)(2-4 yr, 4-6 yr)(6-8 yr, 8-10 yr)(Adult XS, S)(M, L)(XL, 2XL, 3XL)
```

Use a sizing table for finished measurements and a separate table or inline notation for stitch counts. See the [sizing style guide](/style guide/sizing/) for formatting conventions.
