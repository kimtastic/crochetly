---
title: Converting patterns between crochet and knitting
description: A practical guide to adapting a pattern from one craft to the other, covering gauge conversion, yardage estimation, and stitch pattern substitution
---

Found a pattern you love but it is written for the other craft? You can adapt it. Converting between crochet and knitting takes planning, but it opens up more pattern options regardless of which craft you prefer.

## Gauge conversion

The first step is understanding how your gauge changes between crafts. Even with the same yarn and the same needle or hook size, crochet and knitting produce different gauges.

As a starting point, divide the knitting stitch gauge by roughly 1.5 to get a comparable crochet stitch gauge.

**Example:** A knitting pattern calls for 20 stitches per 4 inches in worsted weight yarn. Divide 20 by 1.5 to get 13-14 single crochet stitches per 4 inches. Adjust hook size up or down until you match the target gauge.

The [gauge and tension](/terms/gauge-and-tension/) page covers swatching techniques that apply to both crafts.

## Yardage conversion

Crochet uses more yarn than knitting for the same project. See the [yarn usage comparison](/reference/yarn-usage-comparison/) page for detailed estimates.

The general rule: when converting a knitting pattern to crochet, add 25% to 30% to the yardage. When converting crochet to knitting, subtract about 25%.

**Example:** A knitting pattern specifies 400 yards. Buy 500 yards for crochet. A crochet pattern specifies 600 yards. Buy 450 yards for knitting.

Always buy an extra skein if you are unsure. Yardage estimates are guidelines, not guarantees.

## Substituting stitch patterns

There is no exact one-to-one mapping between knit and crochet stitches. The table below shows reasonable substitutions for common stitch patterns.

| Knitting stitch | Crochet equivalent | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Stockinette | Single crochet in the round | Both produce smooth fabric on the right side. Worked flat, both have a different wrong side |
| Garter stitch | Single crochet in rows | Both produce ridges. Garter stitch is squishier; sc is denser |
| Ribbing (K1P1, K2P2) | Single crochet through back loop only | Creates stretchy ridges but does not match the elasticity of knit ribbing |
| Seed stitch | Single crochet, alternating front and back loop | Produces a bumpy texture similar to seed stitch |
| Lace pattern | Chain spaces and double crochet clusters | Openwork patterns transfer well with design adjustments |
| Cable | Front post treble crochet crossed | Simulates cable look but is thicker and less flexible |

## Fabric considerations

Crochet fabric is thicker and less stretchy than knit fabric. This affects how the finished item fits and behaves.

**Garments:** A crochet sweater will be denser and less flexible than a knitted one. Consider sizing up or choosing patterns with simple shaping. Cardigans and open-front styles work better than pullovers.

**Accessories:** Crochet hats are less stretchy. Make the circumference 1 to 2 inches larger than head size to compensate.

**Blankets:** Crochet blankets are heavier. The weight adds coziness but can make large blankets cumbersome. Consider using a larger hook and a looser stitch pattern to keep the fabric lighter.

**Home decor:** This is where crochet shines. The firm fabric is ideal for baskets, plant holders, and coasters. Converting knitting patterns for home decor items is straightforward.

## When does conversion work best?

Some patterns convert more easily than others.

**Good candidates for conversion:**
- Simple shapes with minimal shaping
- Geometric designs (blankets, scarves, shawls)
- Items where gauge is flexible (throws, accessories)
- Patterns using simple stitch repeats

**Difficult or impractical conversions:**
- Intricate colorwork with complex charts
- Garments with fitted shaping (set-in sleeves, darts)
- Cable patterns where the cable structure is the main feature
- Socks (knit fabric is required for comfort inside shoes)

## When should you design from scratch instead?

If a pattern requires extensive conversion, you may be better off designing from scratch. This is often the case for fitted garments, complex colorwork, and patterns where the stitch pattern defines the item.

The skills you develop from converting simpler patterns will help you learn to design. Start with small, geometric projects and work up to more complex conversions.
