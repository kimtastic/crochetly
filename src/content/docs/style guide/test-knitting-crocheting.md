---
title: Test knitting and crocheting
description: How to plan and run a pattern test call, find testers, collect feedback, and incorporate results into your published pattern
---

Pattern testing is the step between tech editing and publication. Testers make your pattern from start to finish and report back on what worked, what did not, and whether the final item matches the intended result.

## What is the difference between tech editing and testing?

Tech editing and testing serve different purposes, and both are valuable.

A tech editor checks the pattern itself: the math, the abbreviations, the clarity of instructions. They work through it on paper or in a spreadsheet.

Testers actually make the item. They verify sizing, yardage, and whether the instructions make sense to someone who has not spent weeks writing them.

A pattern benefits from both. Tech edit first, then test, then a final editor pass after testing.

## How do you find testers?

Testers come from many places. Your existing audience on social media or your newsletter is a good place to start. Ravelry groups, [Discord servers](/apps/discord-communities/), and other pattern testing communities are also common sources.

Some designers build a testing team over time by reaching out to makers who test well and asking if they want to be notified about future calls.

## What should you include in a test call?

A clear test call helps potential testers decide quickly whether your pattern is right for them.

- Pattern name and type (crochet, knit, garment, accessory)
- Yarn weight and estimated yardage
- Sizes available
- Skills required
- Deadline for finished item and feedback
- What testers receive (free pattern, discount, yarn support)
- What testers provide (photos, feedback, social media permission)

## What should you provide to testers?

Once testers are chosen, send them everything they need.

- The pattern file (tech edited before you send it)
- Yarn and size requirements
- A deadline
- A feedback form or specific questions
- Clear instructions for how to submit photos and feedback

Some designers also provide swatch guidelines or notes on gauge.

## What makes a good feedback form?

A feedback form keeps responses consistent. Ask the same questions of every tester.

- What size did you make?
- Does the finished item fit as expected?
- Were there any confusing instructions?
- Did you run out of yarn, or have too much?
- Would you change anything about the pattern?
- Can you share photos of the finished item?

Open-ended questions catch things you did not think to ask.

## What should you expect from testing?

Expect honest feedback. That is the point. A tester who says a section is confusing is not being negative. They are telling you something useful.

Expect sizing differences. The same pattern may fit one tester perfectly and run large on another. Pay attention to patterns, not individual outliers.

Expect drop-outs. Not every tester finishes on time or at all. Plan for this by recruiting more testers than you think you need.

## How do you handle tester feedback?

Compile all feedback in one place. Look for patterns. If three testers found the same row confusing, that row needs attention. If one tester had a unique issue, note it and move on.

Apply changes carefully. After you update the pattern based on test feedback, send it back to your tech editor for a final review. Changes after testing can introduce new errors.

## Should you compensate testers?

The standard is a free copy of the finished pattern. Some designers also offer yarn support or a small gift. If a tester goes above and beyond, a thank you in the pattern credits or a discount on future patterns is a nice gesture.

## How do you thank testers?

Credit testers in your published pattern. A tester credits section is standard. It acknowledges their time and helps build a community of people who want to test for you again.
