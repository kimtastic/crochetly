---
title: Schematics and garment diagrams
description: How to create and label garment schematics for knit and crochet patterns, including what measurements to include, labeling conventions, and formatting standards for clear, professional diagrams
---

A schematic is a technical drawing of the finished garment with labeled measurements. It shows the maker what the finished piece should measure in each dimension, making it easier to choose a size and check fit.

Schematics are not decorative illustrations. They are functional diagrams that communicate measurement information clearly.

## What measurements should a schematic include?

Include the measurements that the maker needs to choose a size and verify fit. The exact set depends on the garment type.

### Sweaters and cardigans

- Finished chest / bust circumference (half or full, clearly labeled)
- Body length (from underarm to hem, and optionally from cast-on to hem)
- Sleeve length (from underarm to cuff, and optionally from shoulder to cuff)
- Upper arm circumference
- Yoke depth (for raglan and circular yoke sweaters)
- Neck width (for cardigans with button bands)
- Cross back width (for set-in sleeve sweaters)

### Hats

- Head circumference (finished, not stretched)
- Hat height (from brim to crown)
- Brim width (for folded brims)
- Ear flap length (if applicable)

### Socks

- Foot circumference
- Foot length (from heel to toe)
- Leg length (from cuff to heel)
- Cuff height (if longer than standard)

### Mittens and gloves

- Hand circumference (at widest point)
- Total length (from wrist to tip)
- Thumb length
- Cuff length

### Blankets and shawls

- Total width
- Total length
- Repeat dimensions (if the motif or block has a standard size)

## How should measurements be labeled?

Label each measurement clearly with both inches and centimeters (or the primary measurement unit for your audience). Use arrows or dimension lines to show what is being measured.

- Place measurements on the outside of the garment outline.
- Use a straight line with arrows at both ends to indicate the span.
- Write the measurement next to the line: e.g., `20 in / 51 cm`.
- Label half-measurements when showing flat schematics of a garment worked in the round. State clearly: "shown flat, measured in the round as double."

**Example:** A sweater body shown flat with a chest measurement of 21 in / 53 cm means the full circumference is 42 in / 106 cm.

## What is the standard layout for a schematic?

Keep the layout simple and consistent.

- Front of garment shown in the center (or front and back shown side by side).
- Single view is sufficient for simple designs. Add a back view if the back has different shaping or details.
- Show the garment flat and unwrinkled, not on a body.
- Include a scale reference if helpful (e.g., a 4 in / 10 cm bar), though not required if measurements are clearly labeled.
- Use a solid line for the garment outline. Use dashed lines for fold lines, darts, or internal details.

## How do I handle schematics for multiple sizes?

Show the measurements for each size in the schematic or in an accompanying table.

- **Simple approach:** List measurements for the smallest, middle, and largest size directly on the schematic. Use parenthetical notation: `20 (24, 28) in / 51 (61, 71) cm`.
- **Table approach:** Use a separate measurement table and label the schematic with general dimensions. This is cleaner for patterns with many sizes.
- **Range approach:** For multi-size patterns, show the measurement range (e.g., `20-28 in / 51-71 cm`) on the schematic and refer to the sizing table.

## What are common schematic mistakes?

Avoid these issues when creating schematics.

- **Missing measurements:** The maker should not need to calculate missing dimensions. Include every measurement needed to choose a size.
- **Unlabeled half vs. full circumference:** A flat schematic of a tube shows half the circumference. Label it clearly so the maker does not misinterpret the finished size.
- **No stitch pattern indication:** If a cable panel or colorwork motif changes the fabric width, note that the schematic measurement includes the pattern or is measured at a specific point.
- **Inconsistent units:** Use the same unit (inches or centimeters) throughout. Convert between units accurately. Use a conversion rate of 1 in = 2.5 cm.
- **Wrong scale:** An oversized or undersized schematic is misleading. Keep the proportions roughly correct so the maker can visualize the garment.

## What tools can I use to create schematics?

- **Vector graphics software:** Adobe Illustrator, Inkscape (free), or Affinity Designer produce clean, scalable schematics.
- **Drawing tools:** Even a simple hand drawing scanned and digitized works. The key is clear labeling, not artistic skill.
- **Ravelry schematic generator:** Some designers use Ravelry's built-in tools for basic schematics.
- **Grid paper:** Sketch the schematic on grid paper at a consistent scale (e.g., 1 square = 1 in) before creating the digital version.

## What format should a schematic be for the web?

- Export as a PNG or SVG file. SVG scales cleanly at any size.
- Keep the file size small (under 200 KB) for fast loading.
- Use a resolution of at least 72 DPI for digital use.
- Use high contrast (black lines on white background). Avoid light gray lines that are hard to read on screens or in print.
- Include alt text describing the garment and measurements for accessibility.
