---
title: Working with a pattern tech editor
description: How to collaborate with a pattern tech editor from preparing your files to handling corrections, including insights into how editors approach your pattern and common communication tips
---

A pattern tech editor reviews your pattern before it reaches testers or the public. They check math, gauge, sizing, abbreviations, and clarity. This page covers how to work with one effectively.

## When should you get a pattern tech edited?

Before testing. Sending a pattern to testers with obvious errors wastes everyone's time. The editor catches those issues first so testers can focus on making the item and verifying fit, not flagging typo-level mistakes.

After testing, have your editor do a final review. Testers may find issues that require rewrites, and changes after testing can introduce new errors. A final pass catches those.

## What does a tech editor check?

Editors review your pattern from several angles:

- **Stitch counts** — every row or round adds up to the right total
- **Yardage estimates** — realistic for each size
- **Gauge** — consistent with the stitch pattern and yarn weight
- **Sizing charts** — measurements are correct and complete across all sizes
- **Abbreviations** — used consistently, all defined
- **Clarity** — instructions make sense on the first read
- **Schematics** — match the written instructions
- **Terminology** — US or UK terms used consistently, not mixed

## How do you communicate what you need?

Be upfront with your editor about the type of edit you want. Some designers want a full line-by-line math check. Others are confident in their math but want feedback on clarity and flow. Tell your editor what you are unsure about.

Different pattern types need different levels of review. A simple scarf needs less scrutiny than a multi-size garment with short rows, cables, and colorwork. Let your editor know what the pattern involves so they can plan their time.

## How do you send and share files?

Decide on file format before you send anything. Some editors work in Google Docs. Others mark up a PDF. A PDF is common for paid patterns because the editor can add comments and markup directly.

Whatever format you choose, send a single file, not drafts scattered across emails. Name your files clearly.

## What should you include with your pattern?

Send clear images, charts, and schematics if your pattern uses them. An editor needs to see what the finished item should look like to check your instructions against it. If your chart has an error, they need to compare it to the written instructions.

## How do you keep versions straight?

Version your files with clear names. "pattern-name-v2-for-editor.pdf" tells you and your editor exactly which file is current. Before your editor starts, confirm that the file you sent is the right one.

## How do you handle corrections?

Go through each comment one at a time. Make the changes carefully. If you disagree with a recommendation, talk to your editor about why. They may have a reason you have not considered, or you may have a reason they can accommodate. The goal is a better pattern, not winning an argument.

## What does the process look like from your editor's side?

Understanding how editors work helps you collaborate better.

**Editors start with a full read-through.** They read the whole pattern before commenting. This gives them context so their feedback is consistent across the entire document.

**Editors check math in a separate pass.** Most editors work through your pattern with a spreadsheet or by writing out every row and counting stitches. This is where hidden errors surface.

**Editors flag uncertainty.** If an instruction is unclear, an editor will ask for clarification. This is not a criticism of your writing. It means a maker will also find it unclear.

**Editors want to preserve your voice.** A good editor does not rewrite your pattern in their style. They fix errors and clarify instructions while keeping your voice intact. If an edit changes your meaning or feel, push back.

**Editors appreciate clear communication.** If you need to delay, tell them. If you have a deadline, say so upfront. Editors plan their time around projects, and surprises on either side make the work harder.

## What should you look for in a tech editor?

Experience matters. Look for an editor who works in your craft and understands your pattern style. Crochet editing and knitting editing are different skills. A good crochet editor may not catch the nuances of knit stitch mount, and vice versa.

Turnaround time varies. Ask before committing.

## What does tech editing cost?

Rates vary by pattern complexity and length. Simple patterns with minimal sizing cost less. Multi-size garments with charts and schematics cost more. Discuss pricing before work begins so both sides know what to expect.
