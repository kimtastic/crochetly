Knitting styles differ mainly by which hand holds the yarn, how tension is controlled, and which hand does the active movement. No single style is correct. The best one is whatever feels comfortable and allows you to knit without pain.

## How do different knitting styles differ?

The main differences between styles are: which hand carries the yarn, whether the yarn is thrown or picked, and how much the hands move. All of them produce the same stitches. The difference is in speed, ergonomics, and personal preference.

- **Yarn hand:** Right hand (English) or left hand (Continental, Norwegian).
- **Movement:** Throwing the yarn over the needle or picking it with the needle tip.
- **Anchor:** Some styles anchor one needle (lever, Shetland belt) for less hand movement.

## English (throwing)

The yarn is held in the **right hand**. The right hand releases the needle, wraps the yarn over the tip (throws it), then catches the needle again to pull the loop through. English is common in the US and UK and is often the first style taught in English-language resources.

- Yarn in the **right hand**, tensioned over the index finger or through the fingers.
- The right hand **throws** the yarn over the needle tip.
- Can be slower because the right hand releases the needle with each stitch.
- **Best for:** Beginners, knitters who prefer separate hand movements.

## Continental (picking)

The yarn is held in the **left hand**. The right needle picks the yarn through the stitch without letting go of either needle. Continental is common in mainland Europe and is often faster than English.

- Yarn in the **left hand**, tensioned over the index finger.
- The right needle tip **picks** the yarn through the stitch.
- Faster than English. No releasing and regripping the needle.
- **Best for:** Speed, colorwork (one color per hand), knitters with right-hand fatigue.

[Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/results?search_query=continental+picking+knitting+style)

## Norwegian (continental flick)

A variant of Continental where the yarn is held on the **left index finger** with a distinctive flicking motion. The finger flicks forward to bring the yarn within reach of the needle tip. Norwegian is commonly used in Scandinavian knitting.

- Yarn on the **left index finger**, held high and flicked toward the needle.
- The left index finger does most of the work. The right hand barely moves.
- Keeps tension very even, which is useful for stranded colorwork.
- **Best for:** Colorwork, knitters who want even tension with minimal hand movement.

[Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/results?search_query=norwegian+knitting+style)

## Portuguese

The yarn is tensioned around the **back of the neck** or through a **Portuguese knitting pin** (a magnet or pin clipped to the left shoulder). The left thumb or palm flicks the yarn forward to form stitches.

- Yarn around the neck or through a pin, held in front of the work.
- The left hand makes small flicking movements. No gripping or throwing.
- Very ergonomic; often recommended for knitters with arthritis or hand pain.
- Works for both knit and purl with minimal hand movement.
- **Best for:** Knitters with hand or wrist pain, ergonomic knitting.

[Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/results?search_query=portuguese+knitting+style)

## Lever (Irish cottage / flicking)

The right needle is **anchored**. Tucked under the arm, into a belt, or held against the body. The right hand flicks the yarn over the stationary needle tip. This is one of the fastest knitting styles.

- Right needle **anchored** under the arm or in a belt.
- The right hand flicks the yarn over the stationary needle tip.
- Very fast. The right hand moves only the yarn, not the needle.
- **Best for:** Speed knitters, knitters who produce large quantities (sweaters, blankets).

[Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/results?search_query=lever+irish+cottage+knitting+style)

## Combination (Eastern / combination)

A Continental-based style where the **purl stitch is wrapped clockwise** instead of counter-clockwise (standard). This changes the stitch mount on the needle, which the knitter compensates for on the next row. Combination eliminates the need to adjust tension between knit and purl rows.

- Purl stitches are wrapped **clockwise**.
- The stitch mount is opposite after purling. Knit stitches are worked through the back loop to correct it.
- Common in Eastern Europe and among knitters who purl more comfortably with a Continental-style motion.
- **Best for:** Knitters who want smoother knit-purl transitions (ribbing, stockinette).

[Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/results?search_query=eastern+combination+knitting+style)

## Shetland belt knitting

A knitting belt ("makkin belt") with a leather pad is strapped around the waist. The right needle is pushed into the pad to hold it stationary. The left hand holds the other needle and does the active work. This style was developed in Shetland for lace knitting.

- Right needle held in a **belt pad**, stationary.
- Left hand does all the movement. Right hand controls tension.
- Leaves the right hand free for delicate lace work.
- **Best for:** Shetland lace, knitters who want one hand completely stationary.

[Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/results?search_query=shetland+belt+knitting+style)

## Tips for choosing a style

- Try a few styles early in your knitting journey. Switching later is harder.
- Tension consistency matters more than speed. A slower style with even tension beats a fast style with uneven stitches.
- Your dominant hand does not determine your style. Many right-handed knitters prefer Continental (left-hand yarn).
- If a style causes hand or wrist pain, try a different one before giving up knitting.
