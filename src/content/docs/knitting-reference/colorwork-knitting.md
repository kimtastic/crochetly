Colorwork knitting uses multiple colors in a single project to create patterns, motifs, and designs. There are several techniques, each suited to different types of patterns.

## What is stranded colorwork (Fair Isle)?

Stranded colorwork uses two or more colors per row. You carry the unused color across the back of the work as a float. It is worked in the round or flat and is ideal for small repeating patterns.

- Work with **two colors per row** (occasionally more).
- Carry the unused color **loosely across the back** as a float.
- Common for yokes, mittens, hats, and sweaters.
- True Fair Isle is a specific style from the Shetland Islands with small repeating patterns and no more than two colors per row.

### How does gauge differ in stranded knitting?

Stranded fabric is denser than single-color stockinette because the floats pull the fabric in. You may need to go up one or two needle sizes compared to your single-color gauge. Swatch stranded colorwork in the round (not flat) to get an accurate gauge, because purling can change your tension.

### What is color dominance?

In stranded knitting, the two yarns do not sit at the same height on the fabric. The yarn held to the left (or coming from below) floats slightly higher and appears more prominent on the front. This color is said to dominate.

If you want the pattern color to stand out against the background, hold the pattern color on the left. If you want the background to recede and the pattern to blend in, hold the background on the left. Try swatching both ways to see the difference.

In two-handed stranded knitting (one color in each hand), the color held in your left hand generally dominates over the color held in your right hand.

### How do you manage tension in stranded knitting?

Keeping even tension across floats is the biggest challenge in stranded colorwork. Floats that are too tight pucker the fabric. Floats that are too loose snag.

- Stretch the stitches on your right needle as you knit to keep the float long enough.
- Keep the float loose when you switch colors. A tight float is the most common cause of puckering.
- If stretching stitches alone is not enough, put a finger between the work and the float to add extra slack.
- Err on the side of too loose. You can tighten loose stitches during finishing, but puckers from tight floats are much harder to fix.
- Blocking helps even out tension in stranded work.

### How do you work with two colors?

There are several ways to hold two colors in stranded knitting. No single method is best. Pick the one that feels comfortable and gives you consistent tension.

**One color in each hand (English and Continental).** Hold the main color in your right hand and throw it English style. Hold the contrast color in your left hand and pick it Continental style. This is the fastest method because each hand does its own work and you rarely stop.

**Both colors in one hand.** Hold both yarns in your left hand (or right hand) and pick them with your index finger. You must learn to tension two strands independently. Slower than two-handed but useful if you are not comfortable with both styles.

**Norwegian method.** Hold both colors in your left hand. The main color is tensioned over your index finger. The contrast color is tensioned over the middle finger and brought forward when needed. Common in Norwegian stranded knitting and fast once learned.

A few tips make any of these methods work better:

**Practice your non-dominant hand first.** If you normally knit English style, practice Continental (or vice versa) on a small garter stitch project before starting colorwork. This lets you focus on tension without the added challenge of managing two colors.

**Keep each color in the same hand throughout the project.** Switching which hand holds which color makes it impossible to diagnose tension problems. If one color is always looser, you will know which hand is the cause.

**Use your stronger hand for the color there is more of.** Your comfortable hand will give you better tension across the long stretches of main color. Use your unfamiliar hand for the contrasting color, which typically has fewer stitches.

**Do not worry about twisting the yarns together.** If you are consistent about which hand holds which color, the yarns will twist themselves naturally. Only worry about catching floats longer than 5 stitches.

### How do you manage long floats?

Floats longer than 5 stitches should be secured so they do not snag. There are several approaches.

**Catching floats (trapping).** When a run of the same color is about an inch long (roughly 5 stitches for worsted weight), trap the float at the halfway point. Knit up to the middle stitch of the run, then bring the non-working yarn over the working yarn at the back of the work. Knit the next stitch normally with the working yarn. The non-working yarn is now pinned behind that stitch. From the right side, the fabric looks like normal stockinette with no visible catch.

For an even number of stitches, trap as close to the middle as possible. If you need to trap on a purl row, bring the non-working yarn over the working yarn at the back before purling the next stitch — the technique is the same, just done on the purl side.

Rotate the direction you cross the yarns every few catches to prevent tangling. Over a full project, each float should be secured at least once.

**STUART ladderback method.** For very long floats (like a single column of one color against many stitches of another), create a vertical strand on the wrong side called a ladderback. Work a few stitches onto a spare cable or hold them on a stitch holder, then create a loose strand of the unused color behind the gap. Knit the ladderback strand together with your working yarn at regular intervals to lock the long float in place. This is faster than dozens of individual float catches and creates a cleaner wrong side.

**Next-round catching.** Catch the float on the round after it was formed instead of during the colorwork row itself. On the following round, scoop the long float onto the left needle and knit it together with the next stitch. This secures the float without interrupting your colorwork rhythm.

This method works well for floats 6 stitches or wider. For very long floats (10 to 12 stitches or more), catch the same float twice at different points. The technique also lets you fix a missed float up to 2 or 3 rows later, as long as the float has enough slack to avoid puckering.

When using any catching method, avoid stacking the catch points on the same column of stitches. Stagger them across different columns so they remain invisible from the right side.

### How do you fix missed floats after finishing?

If you finish a project and find long floats you missed, you can secure them with a tapestry needle or crochet hook. No need to rip back.

**Weave floats in with a tapestry needle.** Thread the float onto a tapestry needle and weave it through the backs of nearby stitches of the same color, following the path of the fabric. Work in one direction for a few stitches, then double back to lock it in place. This is the same technique as weaving in ends and works with any yarn weight.

**Braid long floats together.** If two floats of the same color run parallel across the wrong side, braid them together loosely. This shortens both floats and keeps them from snagging. Use a tapestry needle to tack the ends of the braid to the fabric so it stays in place.

**Catch floats with a crochet hook.** Use a crochet hook one or two sizes smaller than your knitting needle. From the wrong side, hook the loose float and pull a loop of securing yarn (matching or contrasting yarn) through it. Anchor the loop to the back of a nearby stitch. Repeat at intervals along the float. This works well for very long floats in fabric that is too tight to weave through.

## What is intarsia?

Intarsia uses separate lengths of yarn for each color block. It is worked flat and is best for large motifs like stripes, letters, or geometric shapes. Because each color section uses its own strand, there are no long floats.

- Use a **separate length of yarn** for each color block.
- **Twist yarns at color changes** to prevent holes.
- Worked **flat** (back and forth), not in the round.
- Ideal for large motifs and non-repeating designs.

### How do you twist yarns at color changes?

When switching colors in intarsia, bring the new color under the old one to twist them together. This closes the gap and keeps the fabric connected.

## What is slip-stitch colorwork?

Slip-stitch colorwork uses one color per row. You slip stitches from the row below to create a two-color pattern. It is the easiest colorwork technique for beginners.

- Use **one color per row**.
- **Slip certain stitches** without working them to expose the color below.
- No floats to manage (the slipped stitches carry the yarn vertically).
- Works well for scarves, blankets, and simple patterns.

## What tips help with colorwork?

- **Start with high contrast colors** so the pattern is easy to see. Take a photo of your yarn choices and view it in black and white. If the shades blend together in grayscale, they will not have enough contrast for a readable colorwork pattern.
- **Keep floats loose** by stretching the stitches on the right needle.
- Block your finished piece to even out tension and set the stitches.
- Read charts from **right to left on right-side rows**, left to right on wrong-side rows (flat knitting) or right to left on every round (in the round).
- When working flat in stranded colorwork, every other row is a purl row, which can make tension harder to control. Consider steeking instead of working flat.

## How do you start your first colorwork project?

A good first project makes learning easier. A few choices up front can save frustration.

**Pick a pattern where the main color dominates.** Choose a pattern with one clearly dominant color and a small amount of a contrasting color. Avoid patterns with isolated single stitches in the contrast color, which lack the grip to stabilize their floats.

**Choose wool over slippery yarns.** Sheep wool has microscopic scales and hooks that help entangle the floats and control tension. Slippery yarns (cotton, acrylic, silk, superwash wool, bamboo) make it harder to keep even tension and are better saved for later projects.

**Look for patterns with rest rows.** A row or two of single-color knitting between pattern rows gives the fabric a tension break and reduces density. You will see the floats settle into these plain rows, so the fabric remains balanced.

**Work in the round on circular needles.** Avoid flat knitting (which requires purling in two colors on every other row) and avoid double-pointed needles (where floats get too tight around the corners). A hat-sized tube of about 20 inches on a circular needle creates stockinette fabric with every row worked as a knit, which is the easiest way to learn.

## How do you choose colors for colorwork?

Pick yarns of the **same weight and fiber content** for even fabric. High contrast between colors makes patterns pop. Low contrast creates a subtle, blended look.

- High contrast yields bold, readable patterns.
- Low contrast yields a subtle, tonal effect.
- Use the same yarn weight and fiber for both colors.
- Lights, mediums, and darks all work together depending on the effect you want.

## What supplies do you need for colorwork?

- Main color (MC) and contrast color (CC) or more
- Needles appropriate for your yarn weight (possibly one or two sizes larger than usual)
- Yarn bobbins (for intarsia)
- Tapestry needle for weaving ends
- Stitch markers to track pattern repeats

## What are the major colorwork traditions?

Different knitting traditions around the world have developed their own approaches to colorwork, using distinctive motifs, techniques, and materials.

### Norwegian knitting

Norwegian stranded knitting is known for high-contrast, two-color patterns. Classic motifs include the eight-pointed star and lice patterns (small dots of contrast color scattered across a solid background). Traditional garments include the Mariusgenser (a patterned sweater named after a Norwegian Olympic skier) and the lusekofte (lice jacket). Norwegian knitters often use the Norwegian method of holding yarn, with both colors in the left hand.

### Swedish knitting

Swedish colorwork shares similarities with Norwegian knitting but has its own motifs and history. Bohus knitting, from the Bohuslan region, was developed in the 1930s and 1940s as a cottage industry. It features intricate, fine-gauge stranded colorwork with many colors and very detailed patterns, often on sweaters and cardigans. Traditional Swedish motifs include geometric patterns and stylized flowers.

### Icelandic knitting

Icelandic colorwork is most famous for the lopapeysa, a circular yoke sweater with wide bands of geometric stranded colorwork around the neck and shoulders. Traditional lopapeysa patterns use natural undyed lopi wool in shades of white, grey, brown, and black. The yoke pattern is typically worked from the bottom up, with stranded bands separated by single-color sections.

### Faroese knitting

Faroese colorwork, from the Faroe Islands, often features intricate pattern bands on sweaters and shawls. Traditional motifs include geometric shapes and stylized plant forms. Faroese knitting is known for its use of natural wool colors and the distinctive Faroese shawl shape with a center back gusset.

### Cowichan knitting

Cowichan knitting is an Indigenous tradition from the Coast Salish peoples of British Columbia. Cowichan sweaters are heavy, thick garments knitted from unspun, undyed natural wool in bold graphic patterns. Designs feature animals (whales, deer, eagles), mountains, and zigzag borders. The sweaters are knitted in the round, then cut open (steeked) for the front and sleeves. Cowichan knitting typically uses only natural sheep colors (white, brown, black) and is completely seamless.

### Andean knitting

Andean knitting traditions from Peru and Bolivia are best known for chullo hats with earflaps, knit in the round with bold geometric colorwork. Andean knitters use fine yarns and bright contrasting colors. Traditional motifs include stepped diamonds, zigzags, and stylized animals. The colorwork is typically stranded and very dense, producing warm, windproof fabric.

### Baltic knitting

Baltic knitting traditions from Latvia, Estonia, and Lithuania are renowned for fine-gauge stranded mittens and gloves. Baltic colorwork uses very fine yarn (often fingering or lace weight) and features dense, intricate patterns with many small repeats. A single mitten can contain a dozen or more colors. Traditional Baltic motifs include stars, flowers, and geometric grids. The fabric is knit tightly to create thick, warm, wind-resistant garments.
